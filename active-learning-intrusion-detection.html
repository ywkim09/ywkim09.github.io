Research case study · Cybersecurity · Active learning

From alert overload to analyst-guided intrusion detection.

A research line connecting dynamic alert prioritization with human-in-the-loop learning: focus analyst effort on informative alerts, then use those investigations to detect intrusions sooner.

01 / The challenge

More alerts do not necessarily mean more insight.

Intrusion detection systems can produce a large stream of alerts while an attacker progresses through a network. Analysts have limited time to investigate them. The research question was how to choose which alert to investigate next so that each analyst query improves situational awareness or supports a timely detection decision.

Research problem

Uncertainty about attacker progress

An alert is evidence about possible attacker activity, not a complete view of the system. The detector must reason about uncertainty over hidden states and update that belief as observations arrive.

Operational constraint

Investigation effort is scarce

Asking an analyst to inspect every alert is impractical. Prioritization should therefore account for what is currently uncertain, what can be learned, and whether the accumulated evidence warrants an alarm.

02 / The approach

One research line, two connected steps.

The first study develops active alert prioritization around a Hidden Markov Model (HMM). The follow-up puts analyst investigations in the loop and uses their outcomes to adapt the observation model and support sequential intrusion detection.

Study 1 · Dynamic prioritization

Choose alerts that improve the state estimate

The HMM represents hidden attacker progress and the system's belief about that progress. Active-learning policies select alerts to investigate based on their expected value for updating that belief. The study also reduces the state and exploit spaces to make inference more efficient.

Study 2 · Human-in-the-loop detection

Turn investigation outcomes into detection evidence

Analyst findings from investigated alerts update observation probabilities. The framework combines the resulting evidence with sequential multi-hypothesis testing over pruned HMMs, allowing it to decide when evidence is sufficient to detect an intrusion.

03 / Query strategies

Different policies, different notions of value.

The policies explore complementary ways to spend an analyst query. The names below refer to methods proposed in the two studies; they are not interchangeable objectives.

MaxEntropyChooses alerts expected to reduce uncertainty in the current state belief.
BayesPrioritizes ambiguous alerts under the Bayesian alert-selection approach.
Max RatioSelects investigations to maximize the expected change in likelihood ratios between hypotheses.
Max KLTargets observations that increase distinguishability between competing hypotheses.

The first two policies address alert prioritization and belief estimation; Max Ratio and Max KL are introduced for the human-in-the-loop sequential detection setting.

04 / Findings

Prioritize carefully; keep inference tractable.

In the reported evaluations, active prioritization improved belief estimation over the considered baselines. Dynamic reduction of the modeled state space lowered computation while preserving estimation quality, and the human-in-the-loop policies shortened detection time in simulation under a target false-detection constraint.

Belief estimation

Better use of each labeled alert

  • Proposed prioritization reduced belief MSE by up to 50% versus the evaluated baselines.
  • Dynamic state-space reduction cut computation time by up to 85% without a significant increase in belief MSE.
Sequential detection

Faster decisions with analyst feedback

  • Simulations reported up to 79% lower detection time than a static baseline.
  • The comparison maintained the specified target mean time between false detections.

These are maximum improvements reported in the papers' evaluations, not guarantees for every dataset or operational deployment. The detection-time result is from simulation.
